## 0. Grounding — what campaign 1 actually taught us, restated plainly

Three questionnaire responses, one of them Ana (already-validated, excluded from any "the campaign works" read). Two genuine independent stranger responses, both showing real friction:

- **A puzzle vendor (rompecabezas, 3 products)** stalled at Task 1. Her task-completion grid shows only Task 1 marked — not Task 4 — so her free-text "Registrar ventas" comment most likely describes the onboarding stall, not the sale-registration flow itself. **Flagged, not settled:** this needs raw-data confirmation before being treated as a resolved finding. - **A women's-apparel vendor with 30 products** (roughly 7.5x Ana's own catalog size) said, asked what one thing she'd change: *"no puedo meter tanta ropa porque es demasiada"* — entering her full catalog felt like too much work. She also hit confusion at the NFC tag-assignment step (Task 7).

**What this actually tells us, stated precisely, not overstated:** at n=2, this is not proof of anything at a confidence tier this project would call more than directional (`market-validation.md`'s own n=20/n=100/n=300 bands, and `merchant-validation-decision-matrix.md`'s n=10 correction, both apply the same discipline here). What it *is* — a real, concrete signal that a single generic campaign, sending everyone to the same welcome screen with the same static ad, can't distinguish a small-catalog vendor's ease-of-use question from a large-catalog vendor's does-this-save-me-time-at-scale question. Those are different hypotheses (`market-validation.md` H2's own distinction between catalog-capping-as-control-problem and catalog-capping-as-capital-problem is the adjacent precedent), and a campaign that can't tell them apart can't cleanly validate either. That's the actual problem this document exists to solve.

## 1. Strategic Recommendation

**Move from one generic recruitment campaign to a small set of parallel, hypothesis-specific video campaigns — each targeting a distinct merchant profile, testing one named product assumption, using only the existing interactive prototype, and deep-linking (functionally, see §7) toward the part of the prototype that actually bears on its hypothesis.**

This is a Product Discovery move, not a marketing move: the objective is maximizing validated learning per participant reached, never clicks, demo traffic, or "people liked it." Every campaign below states a falsifiable hypothesis (the same shape `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §9 already established for the Meta Ads Learning Agenda), names the decision it feeds, and states honestly what would invalidate it.

**A critical constraint stated up front, before the campaign list: sequence, don't parallelize everything at once.** Campaign 1's entire organic + paid effort produced two genuine independent responses. Launching five or six parallel video campaigns simultaneously — each needing its own respondents to say anything even directionally — would fragment an already-thin recruitment funnel into segments too small to read at any confidence tier this project holds itself to. §2 and §10 below recommend two campaigns first (Large Catalog, Quick Sale — the two with the strongest existing evidence and the clearest link to backlog priority #1), not the full set at once. This isn't a scope-reduction out of caution alone; it's the same reasoning `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §6 already applied to ad sets ("fragmenting an already-small budget across ad sets is one of the most common reasons a small campaign never gets enough delivery data to say anything useful") — applied here to campaigns instead of ad sets. **This sequencing call is now independently corroborated by `knowledge-mentor`'s Q1 findings — see §3.**

**What doesn't change:** the non-monetary incentive structure (`merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 4), Product Truth discipline, brand voice, and the existing questionnaire's shared core. What changes is recruitment/creative strategy (video, not static; segmented, not generic) and prototype-entry strategy (guided toward the relevant flow, not a single shared welcome screen) — both scoped to *this stage's* actual job, Product Discovery preparation, not advertising.

## 2. Campaign Architecture

Five hypothesis-specific campaign concepts, each fully specified below (§2.1-§2.5); **§1's sequencing recommendation and §10's roadmap make explicit that only two run first.**

**Shared architecture across every campaign:**
- One 20-30 second vertical video, hook → prototype demonstration (using only what's actually built) → explicit hypothesis statement → honest prototype disclosure.
- One landing/expectation-setting touchpoint (an addition to Demo Mode's existing welcome screen, or a campaign-specific variant of it — see §7) telling her, before she starts, which part of the prototype this campaign is asking her to try.
- One questionnaire response: the existing 17-question instrument's shared core (Section A screening, Section D pricing, Section E close/opt-in) stays identical across every campaign for comparability; a short campaign-specific module (Section B's task-completion checklist, scoped to what that campaign's guided flow actually asked her to do) varies by campaign. Full architecture in §8.
- One campaign-identifying self-report question, since the Google Form has no way to detect which ad or video a respondent arrived from (the same accepted limitation `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §7 already names for Landing Page Views) — see §8's "campaign identification" note.

### 2.1 Campaign A — Catálogos grandes (Wave 1)

- **Target merchant profile:** apparel/accessories vendors with large catalogs (roughly 20+ distinct products) — the profile the 30-product respondent belongs to.
- **Hypothesis (H2-adjacent, sharpened by the real finding):** for a large-catalog vendor, the adoption question isn't "is this easy to use" — it's "does this actually save me time once my catalog is this big." Catalog-entry effort and ongoing tag-based selling speed are two *separate* sub-hypotheses this campaign has to keep distinct (see the honest tension named below).
- **A named tension, stated rather than glossed over:** the Product Owner's own suggested flow (choose prepaid → open NFC merchandise flow → assign tags → simulate registration → simulate a sale) starts *after* catalog entry is already done — it tests whether tag-based selling feels fast at scale, not whether entering a 30-item catalog feels like too much work (the actual complaint on file). Both are real, both matter, and they're different questions. **Recommendation:** use the existing seeded "Ver un ejemplo" data (`demoSeed.ts`) to skip manual catalog entry for the video's own demonstrated flow — showing tag-based selling speed is the sharper, cleaner hypothesis to demonstrate on video — but keep an explicit questionnaire item asking her to imagine or briefly attempt entering *her own* catalog size, so the entry-effort question isn't silently dropped just because the video skips it. §8 details this split.
- **Video concept, opening hook:** "¿Vendes muchísimos productos en bazares?" — direct, names the profile immediately, no generic "¿tienes un negocio?" framing.
- **Prototype flow shown:** existing seeded catalog → activar plan de pago (Configuración) → Asignar Tags queue → simulate a tag scan during a sale → digital receipt.
- **Hypothesis statement, on-screen/voiceover, mid-video:** "Estamos explorando una forma más rápida de manejar catálogos grandes." (Product Owner's own phrasing — kept verbatim, it's honest and doesn't overclaim.)
- **Closing disclosure:** "Este es un prototipo interactivo. Queremos saber si esta forma de trabajar resolvería un problema real para tu negocio." (Product Owner's own phrasing, kept verbatim.)
- **Product Truth check:** NFC "scans" are simulated taps, not real hardware reads (`merchant-validation-campaign.md`'s own Product-truth fact #1) — must stay disclosed, not glossed over, exactly as the existing campaign already does.
- **Expected learning:** whether large-catalog vendors perceive tag-based bulk selling as solving a real time cost (a concrete named use case, not "está bien"); separately, whether catalog-entry effort recurs as a distinct blocker even when the demonstrated flow itself lands well.
- **Success criteria (directional, not a fixed bar — same discipline as `market-validation.md`'s H1-H6):** a meaningful share of large-catalog respondents naming time-savings unprompted in the campaign-specific value question; catalog-entry-effort friction, if it recurs across respondents independently of how the video framed the demo, is itself a finding worth escalating regardless of how positively the tagging flow itself was received.

### 2.2 Campaign B — Venta rápida (Wave 1)

- **Target merchant profile:** any bazaar vendor, small-to-medium catalog — the broadest H1 population, not narrowed by catalog size.
- **Hypothesis (H1, the core thesis, never before tested with a video-guided walkthrough):** registration feels fast enough not to compete with attending the next customer.
- **Video concept, opening hook:** "¿Se te va el cliente mientras intentas anotar la venta?" — names the actual friction (customer walking away) instead of a generic "lleva el control de tus ventas" framing.
- **Prototype flow shown:** Home (Idle) → Iniciar Sesión Rápida → Selling grid, 2-3 taps registering a sale → digital receipt.
- **Hypothesis statement:** "Estamos probando si registrar una venta puede ser tan rápido que no te haga perder al siguiente cliente."
- **Closing disclosure:** the same honest "prototipo interactivo, queremos saber si esto resolvería algo real" framing, adapted to this hypothesis.
- **Expected learning:** a real, video-guided comparison point against the existing organic/static-ad cohort's own Task 4 completion — the same before/after logic `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §9 Hypothesis C already applies to the OTP-bug fix, applied here to guided-vs-unguided exposure.
- **Success criteria:** Task-equivalent completion rate among this campaign's respondents, read directionally against the two real completions already on file (a genuinely thin baseline, stated honestly, not treated as a real comparison group).

### 2.3 Campaign C — Control de fin de bazar (Wave 2)

- **Target merchant profile:** vendors who don't currently have a clear picture of what they sold/have left at day's end.
- **Hypothesis:** vendors value an honest running/closing summary (Resultados) enough for it to change their end-of-day behavior — a distinct question from registration speed (Campaign B) even though both touch the same core loop.
- **Video concept, opening hook:** "¿Terminas el bazar sin saber exactamente cuánto vendiste?"
- **Prototype flow shown:** an active Session with a few sales already registered → Cerrar jornada de venta → Close-summary screen → Resultados (Free-tier view).
- **Hypothesis statement:** "Estamos probando si ver tu resumen del día te ayuda a llevar mejor el control de tu negocio."
- **Expected learning:** perceived value of Resultados as an honest end-of-day source of truth — a concrete named use case, not a generic "me gusta ver números."
- **Success criteria:** directional, same confidence discipline as every other campaign here.

### 2.4 Campaign D — Clientes frecuentes (Wave 2)

- **Target merchant profile:** vendors who already informally track repeat customers via Instagram/WhatsApp follows (H3's own target population).
- **Hypothesis (H3):** segmentation is a wanted upgrade once registration is solved, not a standalone draw on its own.
- **Video concept, opening hook:** "¿Sabes quién de tus clientes te compra seguido, aunque sea poquito?"
- **Prototype flow shown:** Paid-tier receipt → Claim Token QR.
- **A Product Truth disclosure this campaign specifically needs, stated in full because it's easy to accidentally overclaim here:** per `merchant-validation-campaign.md`'s own Product-truth fact #2, "Tus clientes" and the Recompensas screens exist and are visually correct, but *permanently* show their honest empty state — nothing a customer does through the QR flows back to the merchant's own view yet. This is a **Planned/Experimental** capability, not an Available one, and the video's own voiceover has to say so plainly (not just leave it to fine print) — something in the register of "estamos probando cómo se sentiría reconocer a tus clientes que regresan; todavía no está conectado de principio a fin." Never show "Tus clientes" populated with real-looking data — the empty state itself is what's honest to show.
- **Hypothesis statement:** "Estamos probando si reconocer a tus clientes frecuentes es algo que de verdad usarías."
- **Expected learning:** whether merchants react positively to the *concept* even knowing the loop isn't closed yet — a genuinely different signal from "does this work," worth reading as its own thing.
- **Success criteria:** perceived-value signal despite the disclosed limitation, read directionally.

### 2.5 Campaign E — Varios clientes a la vez (candidate for Wave 3, not scoped for production yet)

- **Target merchant profile:** vendors who describe simultaneous-customer chaos (several people asking about the same item at once, someone leaving and returning) — the exact scenario Q7's "workflow/mental-model fit" answer option already names.
- **Hypothesis:** the interaction model's pace, not any single step's cost, is what determines fit for vendors whose real bazaar rhythm doesn't give them a calm gap between customers.
- **Video concept, opening hook:** "¿Se te juntan varios clientes al mismo tiempo?"
- **Prototype flow shown:** the existing deliberate-interruption task (start registering a product, switch to Vender sin guardar, return to Inventario) — already validated content from `merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 2 Task 3, never before shown via guided video.
- **Why this is listed but not sequenced into Wave 1/2:** it tests a real, already-named hypothesis (Q7's own codebook category), but doesn't yet have a concrete finding behind it the way Campaigns A and B do — it's a candidate worth keeping on the roadmap, not a priority call this document is making now. Named here so it isn't lost, not because it's ready to produce.

## 3. Product Discovery Rationale

The core discipline this section applies: **test what's already hypothesized, don't invent new speculative hypotheses just because a new channel (video) is available.** Every campaign in §2 maps directly onto an existing, already-stated hypothesis in `market-validation.md` (H1 → Campaign B; H2, sharpened by the real 30-product finding → Campaign A; H3 → Campaign D) or an existing questionnaire construct already built to catch a specific signal (Q7's workflow/mental-model-fit category → Campaign E). None of these are new speculative bets dressed up as campaigns — they're the existing, already-reasoned-through ICP hypotheses, given a sharper instrument (video, deep-linked entry) to test them with.

This matters because of Lean Startup's own build-measure-learn discipline (already the explicit frame `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §9 applies to its own Learning Agenda): a falsifiable hypothesis needs a clean way to be falsified. A single generic campaign sending every respondent to the same welcome screen can't cleanly falsify H1 versus H2 versus H3 — a respondent's reaction is contaminated by whichever screen she happened to land on and whatever mental model she brought to it, not by the specific thing being tested. Segmenting by hypothesis is what makes each campaign's result actually attributable to the thing it claims to test.

**The real risk this section has to name honestly, not just the upside:** JTBD segmentation done well requires enough respondents *per segment* to say anything, even directionally. At n=2 total genuine responses from the first campaign, five parallel segments each needing their own handful of respondents is a real risk of ending up with five inconclusive n=1 reads instead of one directionally-useful n=5-10 read. This is exactly why §1/§10 recommend sequencing two campaigns first, not all five — a JTBD framing that's methodologically sound in principle can still be operationally wrong at this project's actual current reach.

**Update, post-consultation (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** no single tier fully resolves this question, but all of them converge on the same read — this is a volume problem, not a segmentation-design problem. Project Foundation's own directional-only discipline (the n=10/20/100/300 bands cited above) already treats any small n as pattern-level, not proof, regardless of how it's segmented — segmentation was never what determined confidence tier here. A Learning Resources survey-methodology source shows the *statistical* sample-size bar (n≈384) neither the unsegmented nor the segmented approach was ever going to clear, which reframes the real question as whether segmentation preserves *directional/qualitative* signal, not statistical power — a bar this project was never trying to reach. A second Learning Resources source's research-method taxonomy places this campaign shape (a guided task sequence plus a completion checklist) on the *qualitative usability-test* side, not the survey side — a different, lower sample-size logic applies than the n≈384 figure would suggest. General-knowledge qualitative-usability-research guidance (roughly five respondents per homogeneous segment before diminishing returns) suggests the real problem with campaign 1's n=2-per-segment read is that it sits *below even the qualitative floor* — not that segmentation itself is unsound. Classic JTBD theory adds one distinct methodological nuance worth naming honestly, even though it isn't something this document introduces: true JTBD segments by *job*, not by *profile* — H1-H3 (and, by extension, Campaigns A-E) are already profile-shaped, a framing choice made upstream in `market-validation.md`, not something corrected here. **Net: §1/§10's sequencing recommendation (two campaigns first, not all five) is independently corroborated as the correct mitigation for the actual risk — funnel volume, not segmentation design — not contradicted by any tier consulted.**

## 4. UX Rationale

**Does deep-linking reduce abandonment?** Very likely yes, for the specific reason the puzzle vendor's stall illustrates: a generic welcome screen followed by a full, un-guided onboarding asks a respondent to figure out on her own which part of the product is relevant to whatever hook brought her there. A respondent who clicked a video specifically about "catálogos grandes" and then lands on the identical cold-start screen every other respondent sees has to do real interpretive work to connect the video's promise to what she's now looking at — exactly the kind of friction `global-principles.md`'s "the fastest interaction is the one that never happens" already argues against. Landing her closer to the flow the video just showed her removes that interpretive gap.

**Does it bias the experiment?** This is the real, honest risk, not a hypothetical one. Showing only the happy path for one hypothesis, pre-seeded and guided, risks manufacturing a more favorable reaction than an un-guided walkthrough would produce on its own — she never has to discover the flow, so her reaction says less about discoverability and more about the flow's value once found. This is the second flagged consultation question in this document's header — whether this is an acceptable, even necessary, tradeoff (isolating value-perception from discoverability-friction, which is itself useful to separate) or whether it silently inflates positive signal. Stated as an open tension, not resolved unilaterally here.

**Update, post-consultation (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** Foundation constrains *how* any resolution must look (guide, never block — already the conclusion below) but has no content on experimental-bias methodology itself; Learning Resources supplies three real mechanisms that make this tradeoff legible rather than staying a hypothetical concern. **Framing** — curating only the happy path is itself a framing choice, and framing measurably shifts subjective evaluation independent of the thing being evaluated. **The Aesthetic-Usability Effect** — a guided flow's absence of friction can read as "more usable" than the flow structurally is, precisely because she never has to do the interpretive work a genuine first encounter would require. **The Peak-end Rule** — an engineered clean start and clean end, which is exactly what deep-linking constructs, produces a more favorable retrospective read on its own, independent of the flow's actual quality. A separate Learning Resources source's research-method taxonomy names "task-guided use" as a standard, accepted usability-research quadrant, distinct from unguided/organic discovery — which reframes this section's own conclusion below (separating value-perception from discoverability-friction) as normal, accepted research practice, not a special-case workaround invented for this document. General knowledge offers one mitigation option not otherwise named here: a comparison/control condition — routing a portion of respondents to the unguided full walkthrough, or adding a questionnaire item asking her to estimate unprompted discoverability — which keeps the guided cohort's felt reaction and a discoverability estimate as two separate, comparable data points instead of blending them into one. **Net: no tier consulted disagrees this is a real tradeoff; the literature makes it legible without settling whether it's acceptable at this project's current stage — that stays this agent's own call, and the recommendation below (guide, never block) stands, now on firmer methodological ground.** Worth flagging forward, not adopted here: the control-condition mitigation is a genuine option for a future wave — it adds real cost (recruiting and running a second, unguided cohort) this document isn't proposing for Wave 1.

**Does it increase learning?** Yes, in one specific sense worth naming precisely: it lets the questionnaire ask a much more targeted set of questions (§8) because every respondent in a given campaign did roughly the same thing, rather than however much or little of the product she happened to wander into. That specificity is real learning value — but see the bias risk just named, which is the cost side of the same coin.

**Does it better align expectations?** Yes, directly — this is the clearest, least-contested benefit. A respondent told "prueba cómo funciona el registro rápido" and then shown exactly that has her expectations met; a respondent shown a generic welcome screen and left to find her own way has no such alignment, which is close to what happened with the puzzle vendor stalling at Task 1 before ever reaching anything the campaign was actually trying to learn about.

**Should users see only the relevant flow, or eventually the full prototype?** Recommend: **guide toward the relevant flow first, but never block access to the rest.** This directly follows `character-bible.md`'s "gives her an honest way out of anything — no dead ends" and the existing product's own "no back arrow, nowhere to return to" pattern is about a one-way *informational* gate, not about restricting exploration — those are different things. A large-catalog respondent who, after trying the guided NFC flow, also wants to poke at Resultados should be free to. What changes is that the **questionnaire's task-completion checklist stays scoped to what that campaign's guided flow specifically asked her to do** (§8) — so incidental exploration beyond the guided path doesn't get silently conflated with completing the actual test, but it's also never prevented.

**Whether every campaign should start at the same place — critically evaluated, not assumed:** no. The existing single-entry-point design made sense when there was one generic campaign testing one generic value proposition; it stops making sense once campaigns are hypothesis-specific by design. The one thing that should stay constant across every campaign, deliberately, is **Authentication and Business Onboarding** — not skipped, not seeded past. This is a genuine UX judgment call worth stating explicitly: identity creation and business setup are themselves part of what's being validated (comprehension of the app's very first moments is exactly where the puzzle vendor's stall happened), and skipping past them to reach a "more interesting" flow faster would remove real, relevant signal, not just friction. What's deep-linked is what happens *after* that shared entry — not the entry itself.

## 5. Marketing Rationale

**Why video over static, for this specific purpose (not general marketing best practice):** the puzzle vendor's Task 1 stall and the 30-product vendor's confusion at Task 7 are both discoverability/comprehension problems a static screenshot can't address — she has to construct the flow from a single frozen image and her own guesswork. A short video demonstrating the actual sequence of taps removes exactly that guesswork before she ever opens the prototype herself, which is the single most direct response available to what campaign 1 actually found.

**Cost/effort tradeoff, named honestly rather than assumed away:** multiple distinct campaign videos are a real production cost, and iterating video creative is slower than iterating ad copy. This is precisely why §1/§10 recommend two campaigns first, not five in parallel — not only for the recruitment-funnel-fragmentation reason in §3, but because committing to five distinct video productions before learning anything from the first two risks sinking real effort into hypotheses that may need reframing once real respondent reactions come in. (Section 9 — video production feasibility itself — is explicitly out of this agent's scope per this dispatch; this paragraph names the tradeoff without answering whether it's technically/operationally feasible.)

**Why Campaign A (Catálogos grandes) and Campaign B (Venta rápida) specifically, as the first two:** Campaign A has the freshest, most concrete real evidence behind it (the 30-product vendor's own words) and maps to a genuinely untested product question — does tag-based selling actually save time at scale, a question the existing organic/static campaign never demonstrated at all. Campaign B tests H1, this project's single highest-priority hypothesis (`market-validation.md` H1, `company/backlog.md` #1), and gets a real before/after comparison point against the two existing organic responses' own Task 4 experience — the strongest, most directly comparable learning opportunity available anywhere in this document.

## 6. Experiment Design Rationale

Every campaign in §2 is stated as a falsifiable hypothesis with a named decision it feeds — the same shape `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §9's Learning Agenda already uses (Hypothesis A-D, each with supporting/invalidating evidence). Applying that same discipline here, explicitly:

**Cross-campaign comparison is not apples-to-apples, and this has to be stated plainly to avoid a real reasoning error.** If Campaign A's completion rate is higher than Campaign B's, that's not automatically evidence that the NFC/tagging flow is better-designed than the Quick Sale flow — it could just as easily be an audience-composition effect (large-catalog vendors self-selecting into a campaign that speaks directly to their pain, versus a broader, more heterogeneous Quick Sale audience) or a hook-quality effect (one video's opening line landing better than another's), independent of anything about the flow itself. Any comparison across campaigns has to be read with this confound named, the same way `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §8's Decision Playbook already insists the OTP-bug confound be named every time it could explain an abandonment signal.

**Selection bias, restated for this context:** video-recruited respondents within any one campaign are self-selected for curiosity about that specific hook — the same caveat `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §8 already states for paid, ad-driven volunteers generally ("don't treat the Sí-rate as representative of all itinerant vendors"), applied here per-segment rather than per-channel.

**Confidence discipline, applied identically to every campaign, no exceptions:** every "success criteria" line in §2 is deliberately phrased as directional signal, never a fixed pass/fail bar, per `market-validation.md`'s own confidence-tier discipline and `merchant-validation-decision-matrix.md`'s corrected n=10 margin (~±31pp, not the looser ±20pp figure a naive read of the n=20 band would suggest). At the volumes this project is actually likely to see per campaign, no single campaign's result should be reported as a resolved finding on its own — only as a candidate signal, escalated to Validated status only per `market-validation.md`'s own stated bar (two independent sources), same as every other hypothesis in this project.

## 7. Recommended Prototype Entry Strategy

**Resolved 2026-08-19 — `architect` ruling.** True code-level deep-linking (a URL that drops a participant directly into `Inventario > Asignar tags`, skipping Authentication) was evaluated and found disproportionate for this scope, not merely deferred. It would require: (a) loosening the "no domain write" invariant `demo-mode.md` states as a non-goal and every prior Architecture Review of that document has relied on; (b) editing Merchant Application screen components (`App.tsx`, `InventoryScreen.tsx`) to accept externally-injected navigation state, a direct breach of `decision-log.md` D48's placement discipline (Demo Mode infrastructure never touches Merchant Application flow logic); (c) a new URL-param-to-domain-object seeding layer duplicating, in an unreviewed path, invariants `domain-model.md` already governs elsewhere. `architect`'s explicit recommendation: the guided-instruction baseline below is the correct **permanent** answer for this scale of campaign work, not a stopgap awaiting a future enhancement. If deep linking is ever needed at real scale (many campaigns, frequent new entry points), that's a fresh `architect` dispatch and likely a new decision-log entry in its own right — not something this document keeps open as a pending upgrade.

**Recommendation: keep Authentication/Onboarding as the single shared entry point (per §4's own reasoning — this is real, relevant signal, not overhead to skip), then guide her toward the campaign-relevant flow through explicit, honest instruction.**

This is buildable today with **zero code changes and zero architecture risk**, using only what already exists:
- Demo Mode's welcome screen (`demo-mode.md` §3.3) already supports a campaign-scoped variant in principle — this document proposes a short, campaign-specific addition naming the one thing this particular video showed her and inviting her to go find it (e.g., for Campaign A: "en este prototipo, prueba activar el plan de pago y etiquetar productos con NFC — es lo que viste en el video"). This is copy-only, reusing an already-Approved mechanism, not a new screen or new code path.
- For flows that need a populated catalog to be meaningful (Campaign A specifically — an empty catalog can't demonstrate tag-assignment-at-scale), the guidance explicitly points her toward the already-built "Ver un ejemplo" seeded data (`onboarding.md`'s own existing path), rather than asking her to hand-enter products herself before she can reach the flow the video promised.

**Multiple entry points → what stays common vs. what varies (the "how do we keep maintenance simple, compare results across campaigns" question, addressed directly):**
- **Common, never varies:** Authentication, Onboarding, the questionnaire's Section A (screening), Section D (pricing), Section E (close/opt-in), and the underlying prototype codebase itself — no campaign gets its own fork of the product.
- **Varies per campaign:** the video, the welcome-screen guidance sentence naming which flow to try, and the questionnaire's Section B task-completion checklist (scoped to that campaign's guided flow, per §8).
- **Comparability mechanism:** because Sections A/D/E stay identical, cross-campaign comparison on screening profile, pricing sensitivity, and adoption intent (Q13/Q16-equivalent) remains valid and comparable — it's specifically the *usability/task-completion* read that becomes campaign-scoped and non-comparable across campaigns, which §6 already names as an expected, not a hidden, limitation.

## 8. Questionnaire Strategy — Prototype + Questionnaire as One Experience

**The existing infrastructure this builds on, not duplicates:** `demo-mode.md`'s 2026-08-18 amendment already added a persistent, session-wide Form-reminder banner and a welcome-screen expectation-setting sentence, specifically because the first paid campaign showed almost no one reaching the Form despite real prototype traffic. This section extends that fix for a multi-campaign world; it doesn't propose replacing it.

**1. Stronger CTA inside the video.** Every campaign's closing disclosure (§2's "Este es un prototipo interactivo...") should be immediately followed, in the same breath, by the pairing framing: something in the register of "pruébalo y cuéntanos qué te pareció — las dos cosas nos ayudan por igual." This sets the expectation *before* she ever taps through to the prototype, which is cheaper than any in-app fix, since it never has to compete with her attention once she's already exploring. **Post-consultation (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** this pairing framing is directly reinforced by the Zeigarnik Effect — unfinished/interrupted tasks pull at attention more than completed ones, which supports naming both halves as one activity early, before either is started. The early light-commitment CTA this point represents is separately reinforced by commitment-and-consistency (asking for a small, explicit "yes, I'll try both" commitment up front makes following through more likely).

**2. Welcome-screen framing, extending §3.3's existing closing sentence rather than adding a third one.** `demo-mode.md` already fought hard to compress this screen's body copy from 91 words back to 75 (`ux-critic` DEMO-M2) — any new content here has to earn its place against that same density discipline, not silently reopen it. Recommend folding the "one activity, not two" framing into the *existing* closing sentence rather than appending a new one: e.g., adjusting "No es la versión final — cuéntanos qué se te hace confuso... Más adelante te decimos cómo" to something that names the pairing explicitly without adding net length — this is a copy-level refinement for `ux-designer`/`brand-guardian` to execute precisely, not a full rewrite proposed here. **Post-consultation:** same Zeigarnik Effect grounding as point 1 above — naming the pairing here, at the moment she's about to start the prototype half, reinforces the "unfinished until both halves are done" framing at the second of the two moments it matters most (before starting, and again per point 3's natural-end-state idea).

**3. A transition screen inside the prototype itself — evaluated and not recommended as currently scoped.** `demo-mode.md`'s own architecture already states the honest reason this doesn't have a clean answer today: *"the prototype has no defined 'finish' moment to hook a single reminder to."* For the original generic campaign that's true. For a campaign-specific *guided* flow, though, there genuinely is a natural end-state per campaign (Campaign A: the tag-assignment queue clears; Campaign B: the receipt renders; Campaign D: the QR is shown) — which opens a real, if narrower, possibility: the persistent banner's own copy could adapt once she reaches that specific screen, without becoming a new dismiss-forever mechanic or a first-person "ya terminé" claim (both already rejected by `brand-guardian` for the existing banner, per `demo-mode.md`'s own review record). **This is flagged as a genuine, scoped recommendation for a future RFC/product-decision, not something this document specs or authorizes building** — it touches actual prototype code and screen-identity logic, squarely outside Marketing's remit, and needs `ux-designer`/`architect` to evaluate against `demo-mode.md`'s own D48 placement discipline before anything is designed.

**4. Reducing perceived remaining effort, not shortening the instrument.** The existing 17-question instrument (`merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 3) went through seven remediation passes to reach its current shape — this document does not recommend shortening it unilaterally, the same discipline `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §8 already applies ("don't shorten the already-reviewed Deliverable 3 instrument unilaterally; log this as a candidate finding for a future round"). What's recommended instead: the guidance copy accompanying each campaign's guided flow (§7) can honestly note, once she reaches the flow's natural end, that the questionnaire itself is short and specific to what she just did — reducing the *perceived* remaining effort without touching the instrument's actual length. **Post-consultation (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** this is directly supported by the Goal Gradient Effect — motivation rises as the perceived distance to a goal shrinks, so honestly framing the remaining questionnaire as short and specific (not just short) is exactly the kind of remaining-distance signal this effect predicts will help. **Superseded in part by §11 below:** the "future round" this point deferred a real length change to has now arrived — §11 re-evaluates the instrument's actual length directly, using real (if still thin) completion data that didn't exist when this point was written. This point's own recommendation (honest framing, no unilateral shortening) is not wrong and stays in force for the *current, live* campaign — see §11.6 — but it's no longer the final word on whether the instrument's length itself should ever change.

**5. Campaign-specific questionnaire modules, architecture proposed, full instrument not specified here.** Recommend: Section B's checkbox-grid (`merchant-validation-campaign.md` Q5) gets a campaign-specific variant — a Campaign A respondent sees rows matching the NFC/tagging tasks she was actually guided through, not the full generic eight-row list built for the original campaign's own optional branch. This reuses the exact architecture `merchant-validation-decision-matrix.md` already established (hypothesis → question → threshold, one row per task) — it's an extension of that existing document's pattern, not a new one. **Scope note: fully specifying five campaign-specific modules is a dedicated Deliverable in its own right, not something this strategy document attempts to complete inline** — flagged as the concrete next artifact once Wave 1's two campaigns are approved to proceed (see §10). **Sequencing relative to §11's core/extended restructure: see §11.5 — these are two compatible, not competing, changes, and this document now recommends running them in sequence, not as one coordinated redesign.**

**6. Campaign identification, since the Form has no native source tracking.** Recommend one short, forced-choice screening question added to the shared core (not a campaign-specific module, since every respondent answers it regardless of route): "¿Cuál de estos viste antes de entrar? — Catálogos grandes / Venta rápida / Control de fin de bazar / Clientes frecuentes / Llegué por otro medio." This is the same honest workaround already used elsewhere in this project for an unavoidable Google Forms limitation (`merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §7's own accepted gap: "the Form doesn't carry a source field... a visible spike during the active window... is the practical read") — cheap, self-report, imperfect, but the only available mechanism without new analytics infrastructure.

**7. Completion incentive — reinforce, don't duplicate.** The existing incentive structure already ties priority-list/community access to completing *both* the prototype and the questionnaire (`merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 4). Recommend each video's closing disclosure state this briefly too, rather than leaving it to be discovered only in the Form's own closing text after the fact — consistent with `tone-of-voice.md`'s "state facts before offering an opinion": she should know what completing both gets her before she decides whether to start, not after. **Post-consultation (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** the standing non-monetary structure this point reinforces is itself supported by the Trivialization Effect — people often prefer genuine acknowledgment over a trivial reward, so keeping this non-monetary isn't only ethically cleaner, per this literature it isn't even a motivational sacrifice.

**8. Concurrent interleaving — an alternative structure worth naming, not adopted here (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18).** Points 1-7 above describe a fully sequential structure: finish the guided prototype flow, finish the questionnaire, then receive delayed community/priority-list access. The Temptation Coupling literature suggests pairing tedium with something appealing works best *concurrently*, not sequentially — e.g., interleaving one or two short questionnaire prompts into the guided flow itself, at the natural pause points point 3 above already identifies per campaign (the tag-assignment queue clearing, the receipt rendering, the QR appearing), rather than holding every questionnaire item until after the entire flow is done. This is a genuinely different design — it would require a prompt to appear mid-session, not only after — not a small copy change. Flagged as an alternative design space for a future round; not specified or recommended for Wave 1's already-reviewed instrument.

**9. Immediate, non-monetary acknowledgment at completion — an addition, not currently in this section (`knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18).** Hyperbolic Discounting predicts people heavily discount delayed rewards; the existing community/priority-list access (point 7) is both delayed and non-guaranteed. A mitigation this suggests, additive to the existing delayed framing rather than a replacement for it: something immediate and non-monetary at the exact moment she finishes both halves — e.g., a closing screen or message that genuinely thanks her by name for what she just did, distinct from only pointing forward to a later, uncertain benefit. Scope note, same discipline as point 3 above: this is a candidate for a future copy/product decision, not specified in full here — `ux-designer`/`brand-guardian` would need to design the actual moment and wording.

**Net (Q3, `knowledge-mentor`, 2026-08-18):** points 1, 2, 4, and 7's existing tactics are independently reinforced by the Zeigarnik Effect, the Goal Gradient Effect, commitment-and-consistency, and the Trivialization Effect respectively — none of them needs revision. The standing non-monetary incentive structure specifically is not a motivational sacrifice per this literature, only an ethical improvement over a trivial reward. Points 8 and 9 are genuinely new tactic options this consultation surfaced, offered as an alternative design space worth considering for a future round — not a correction to anything recommended above.

## 9. Video Production Workflow — Assessed Directly by Main, Not `marketing`

This section is deliberately written by Main, not `marketing` — it depends on the actual tool capabilities available in this project's agent environment, not domain expertise, and overstating automation we don't have would violate the same Product Truth discipline this whole document holds every campaign to.

**Honest baseline: no tool in this environment can produce a finished video today.** There is no screen-recording tool, no video-editing/compositing tool, no voice-over/TTS generation tool, and no captioning/subtitle-burning tool available to any agent in this project. `ui-designer` and `merchant-user-tester` both hold browser-automation tools (`chrome-devtools`) capable of navigating the live prototype and capturing individual still screenshots — neither can record continuous motion (taps, transitions, scrolling) into a video file. This is a real capability gap, not a preference; nothing in §2's five campaign concepts can be produced end-to-end by this project's agents alone.

**What genuinely can be automated today, and by which agent:**
- **The full shot list and script** (hook line, on-screen text per beat, voiceover script, closing disclosure, exact taps/screens to demonstrate) — `marketing` already produces this shape of artifact (§2's five campaigns are effectively shot lists already); a dedicated pass per campaign (roadmap step 3) is the next concrete deliverable, not new capability.
- **A precise, ordered screenshot sequence of the exact flow being demonstrated** — `ui-designer`, using its existing `chrome-devtools`/browser-automation access to the running prototype, can capture one still image per beat in the script (e.g. Campaign A: seeded catalog → Configuración → Activar plan de pago → Asignar Tags queue → tag-scan simulation → receipt), each cropped/framed consistently. This is a **storyboard**, not a video — a strong visual reference for whoever records the real screen capture, and independently useful for reviewing whether the proposed flow actually looks the way the script assumes before any recording happens.
- **Caption/subtitle text** — since the script already specifies every on-screen line, the caption track is a byproduct of the script, not a separate production step; no automation needed beyond writing the script well.
- **Copy review** (`brand-guardian`, per roadmap step 4) and Foundation-consistency checks (`reviewer`, if the video's own demonstrated flow ever needs to be checked against an Approved spec) both apply to the script/storyboard artifact the same way they apply to any other participant-facing copy — no new capability required, just the existing review pipeline pointed at a new artifact type.

**What requires the Product Owner's own tooling, stated plainly rather than left implicit:**
- **Actual screen recording** of the live prototype (continuous motion, not stills) — macOS's built-in screen recording (Cmd+Shift+5) or a lightweight capture tool, following the storyboard/script as a shot-by-shot guide.
- **Voice-over** — either the Product Owner's own recorded voice, or an external AI voice tool (e.g. ElevenLabs or similar) — no such tool exists in this project's agent toolset today; adding one would be a genuine, separate tooling decision (and, per `product/00-foundation/architecture-principles.md` principle 8 — the AI-engineering-lab tiebreaker adopted 2026-08-17 — a legitimate candidate for that lens *if* a comparable-cost alternative exists and the need is already genuine, not manufactured for the sake of the exercise).
- **Editing/compositing** (trimming, adding captions/text overlays, transitions, vertical 9:16 formatting for Reels/Stories) — a consumer tool built for exactly this (CapCut is a reasonable default: free, mobile-first, built-in auto-captions, vertical-native templates) is more practical here than anything this project's current agents could build or orchestrate.
- **Publishing** — uploading the finished video into Ads Manager as ad creative, same manual step every prior campaign's creative already went through.

**Recommended hybrid workflow, concretely:**
1. `marketing` writes the full script + shot list per campaign (already the shape of §2's specs).
2. `ui-designer` captures a still-image storyboard from the live prototype matching the script's exact beats, flagging anywhere the real flow doesn't match what the script assumed (a cheap check before any real recording effort is spent).
3. `brand-guardian` reviews the script's voiceover/on-screen copy (roadmap step 4).
4. Product Owner performs the actual screen recording, following the reviewed script/storyboard as a shot-by-shot guide, then edits/captions/formats using an external consumer tool.
5. Main or `marketing` does a final Product Truth check on the edited video before it's approved for publishing — confirming nothing in the final edit implies unfinished functionality exists in production, the same check every prior campaign asset has already gone through.

**Reusable template, so this gets faster over time even without new automation:** every campaign's script already follows one fixed four-beat structure (Hook → Prototype demonstration → Hypothesis statement → Honest disclosure), per §2's own shared architecture. Codifying this as an explicit script template (a fill-in-the-blanks structure `marketing` reuses per campaign) is the actual lever for speed here — not tooling automation this project doesn't have, but a consistent, fast-to-fill format that keeps steps 1-3 above cheap and repeatable as more campaigns are drafted.

**Not recommended right now:** building custom video-generation tooling into this project's agent stack purely to enable this specific campaign wave. The honest cost/benefit doesn't support it at this scale (a handful of 20-30 second videos), and per architecture-principles.md principle 8's own gate, a new tooling investment needs a genuine need and a real cost-comparable alternative before AI-engineering learning value can even apply as a tiebreaker — "we want videos for two campaigns" doesn't clear that bar on its own.

### 11.2 Is a genuinely shorter version viable without losing what actually matters? — No, not as a flat trim; yes, as a tiered structure

**A flat trim (fewer total questions, one pass) is not recommended.** The core problem with cutting the instrument down to, say, 8-10 questions for *everyone* is that it applies the same tradeoff uniformly to every respondent, including the ones genuinely willing to give more. It doesn't target the actual failure mode (people abandoning partway through) — it just throws away signal from engaged respondents to buy a shorter number for disengaged ones, a bad trade when the engaged respondents are exactly the ones Deliverable 4's merit-based pilot selection depends on (§11.4 below). It also permanently sacrifices real, Decision-Matrix-mapped signal (see §11.3) for every respondent, forever, rather than only for the respondents who would have abandoned anyway.

**A two-stage core + optional extended structure is recommended instead** — the shape the Product Owner herself named. Google Forms already supports this natively and cheaply: this exact instrument already uses section-based conditional branching twice (Q17 shown only if Q16 = Sí/Tal vez; Deliverable 2's optional Paid-tier task branch, mirrored in Q5's conditional rows 6-8) — adding one more section break ("ya contestaste lo esencial — si quieres ayudarnos con más detalle, sigue aquí") is structurally the same mechanism applied one more time, not a new capability.

**Proposed core (target: ~2-3 minutes, all forced-choice/checkbox/short-answer, no open paragraphs):**

| # | Question | Type | Est. time | Why it's core |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q2 | privados/tianguis/ambos | MC | ~10s | Feasibility screen |
| Q3 | zona | Short answer | ~15-20s | Feasibility screen |
| Q5 | task-completion checkbox grid | Checkbox grid | ~30-45s | Only usability signal for self-guided sessions |
| Q7 | usability-vs-workflow-fit-vs-expectation-mismatch classifier | MC + otro | ~15-25s | Cheap, makes Q5's gap interpretable |
| Q13 | ¿lo usarías gratis mañana? | Scale | ~10s | Free-tier adoption baseline, own escalation trigger |
| Q16 | opt-in Sí/Tal vez/No | MC | ~10s | The pilot-selection mechanism itself |
| Q17 | contacto | Short answer, conditional | ~15s | Operationally required to act on Q16 = Sí/Tal vez |

Total: ~7 items, roughly 105-140 seconds — genuinely inside a 2-3 minute band, not a loosely-labeled one.

**Proposed extended tier (offered, not required — the remaining ~10-11 items):** Q1, Q4, Q6, Q8, Q9, Q10, Q11, Q12, Q14, Q15 — everything qualitative, the pricing-model preference, and the forced single-change prioritization question. Framed honestly as optional, consistent with `character-bible.md`'s "gives her an honest way out of anything — no dead ends": she can stop after the core and have genuinely completed something real (the opt-in has already happened), or continue.

### 11.3 What's actually lost — named plainly, not papered over

If a meaningful share of respondents stop at the core and never continue, the following Decision-Matrix-mapped signal is lost or weakened for them specifically:

| Lost/weakened | Severity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Q12 — "qué te haría dudar"** | **Most severe.** | This is the campaign's own stated central objective. A campaign that captures opt-ins and task-completion rates but not *why merchants wouldn't adopt* has quietly drifted from a validation instrument toward a recruitment form — precisely the outcome this whole campaign was designed to avoid ("success is not 'people liked it'"). |
| **Q15 — forced single change** | **Severe.** | The Decision Matrix's own account of this question is that it carries *elevated* confidence specifically because it's forced-choice — losing it loses the single sharpest prioritization signal in the instrument, not an average one. |
| **Q9 — missing capability** | Real. | Named directly by the Product Owner as a pattern worth acting on ("merchants repeatedly request the same missing capability"). |
| **Q10 — most-useful feature** | Real. | Also a named Product Owner pattern ("most merchants choose Inventory as first valuable feature → prioritize Inventory"). |
| **Q6, Q8 — confusion / expectation-mismatch detail** | Real, but partially mitigated. | Q5 (task-level pass/fail) and Q7 (root-cause classifier) stay in the core, so some usability signal survives — what's lost is the *specificity* (which exact screen, which exact expectation) that lets `ux-designer` triage a fix precisely rather than just knowing "a usability problem exists somewhere in Task X." |
| **Q14 — pricing model preference** | Real, but partially mitigated. | Q13 (free-tier baseline) stays in the core, so a directional price-sensitivity read survives; the specific flat/percentage/depends preference (H5, `business-decisions.md` Q17) does not. |
| **Q1, Q4, Q11** | Lower. | Q4 and Q11 are the two the Decision Matrix itself already marks as non-independently-triggering; Q1 is a refinement on top of Q2/Q3's screening, not a standalone gate. |

**A second, real interaction this section has to name honestly: Deliverable 4's merit-based selection depends on open-text quality.** Pilot admission and NFC-kit consideration among "Sí" respondents are explicitly merit-based — "the quality of the feedback given during the walkthrough and questionnaire," per Deliverable 4 — and that quality signal today comes almost entirely from the open-paragraph questions (Q6, Q8, Q9, Q11, Q12, Q15), all of which sit in the proposed extended tier. If most "Sí" respondents stop at the core, Nahui loses most of its actual basis for distinguishing a thin "Sí" from a genuinely engaged one — the exact distinction the merit-based structure exists to protect (`merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 4: "a 'Sí' respondent who gives thin or generic answers stays on the list but isn't prioritized"). **This cuts in favor of the tiered design, not against it** — it means the extended tier's own framing can honestly tell a "Sí" respondent that more detail helps her own candidacy specifically ("contarnos más nos ayuda a conocer mejor tu negocio"), which is a true statement, not a manufactured incentive, consistent with `tone-of-voice.md`'s "state facts before offering an opinion." A flat trim would have no equivalent recovery mechanism — the lost merit-signal would simply be gone for everyone.

### 11.4 Interaction with §8 point 5's campaign-specific modules — sequence separately, short-form first

§8 point 5 above proposes that Section B's task-completion grid (Q5, and by extension Q6-Q9) becomes campaign-specific once Strategy v2's video campaigns run — a Campaign A respondent sees NFC/tagging rows, a Campaign B respondent sees Quick-Sale rows. That's a different axis of variation (*which* tasks get asked about) from this section's core/extended split (*how many* questions, structured as mandatory-vs-optional). The two are compatible, not competing.

**Recommendation: sequence them, don't redesign both at once.** The campaign-specific-modules idea is dependent on Wave 1 video campaigns actually being approved and produced — and §9 above is explicit that no video-production tooling exists in this project today, making that a multi-week, uncertain-timeline dependency chain. The core/extended restructure, by contrast, is directly relevant to completion problems happening on the *existing*, already-running instrument right now, independent of whether video campaigns ever launch. Coupling the two means an available, low-risk fix waits on an unrelated, higher-uncertainty initiative — a bad reason to delay.

Concretely: build and validate the core/extended restructure now, against the existing shared (non-campaign-specific) instrument, for the next wave (§11.6). When campaign-specific Section B modules eventually get built, they inherit the already-established core/extended shape rather than each campaign inventing its own structure — a campaign-specific task grid becomes that campaign's own version of the "core" Section B item (Q5-equivalent, short, scoped to what its guided flow showed), with that campaign's own open-text follow-ups (Q6/Q8-equivalent) living in its own extended tier. Getting the core/extended architecture right once, on the simpler existing instrument, is cheaper than inventing it fresh alongside five new campaign modules simultaneously.

### 11.5 Should this apply to the current, still-running Meta campaign's Form? — No. Apply it to the next wave.

The Meta Ads campaign (`merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md`) is live 2026-08-17 through 2026-08-20, MXN $500 total, and its Form link (`forms.gle/ZZhtJEfee3viWY1h8`) is already embedded in running ad copy that respondents have already clicked. Google Forms technically allows editing a live form's structure without changing its URL — so there's no *technical* forcing function requiring a fresh link. The reasons against editing now are practical and evidentiary, not technical:

1. **Nearly-exhausted runway, small marginal benefit.** With the campaign roughly two-thirds through its 3-day window as of this writing, the number of not-yet-arrived respondents who could benefit from a shorter core is small — the disruption risk below is being taken on for a thin remaining slice of the campaign's own total reach.
2. **Mid-flight structural edits risk breaking respondents already in progress.** Someone with the Form open in a browser tab, partially filled, may hit an inconsistent state if the question structure changes underneath her mid-session — a real risk to exactly the respondents this change is trying to help, not a theoretical one.
3. **Stacking a second, unremoved confound onto an already-confounded read.** `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md`'s own Background section already establishes that this campaign's results have to be read carefully against the OTP-bug history; introducing a second mid-campaign structural change (question count/order) makes any pre/post split within this same campaign's own data nearly uninterpretable — a lower completion rate before the edit and a higher one after could be paid-reach composition, the edit itself, or noise at this sample size, with no clean way to separate them.
4. **This is exactly the precedent both source documents already set.** `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §8's Decision Playbook: "Don't shorten the already-reviewed Deliverable 3 instrument unilaterally; log this as a candidate finding for a future round," and "a future round should test a shorter or optional path, not a reason to change the current, already-approved instrument mid-flight." Both explicitly anticipated this exact situation and pointed at the next round, not the current one — nothing about the new evidence in this section changes *when* that's the right call, only *what* the next round should look like.

**Recommendation: do not modify the live Google Form during the current campaign window.** Let the current wave finish and close as designed. Prepare the core/extended restructure (§11.2) for the *next* round — whichever the Product Owner decides that is: a re-run of the same generic organic/paid campaign, or Wave 1 of Strategy v2's hypothesis-specific video campaigns (§2). Either way, the restructured instrument should go live as a fresh start to a new recruitment wave, not as a mid-course correction to one already in flight.

### 11.6 Recommendation summary — for the Product Owner's decision, not executed here

1. **A flat trim of the 17-question instrument is not recommended.** It sacrifices real signal (most severely, Q12 and Q15) for every respondent uniformly, including ones willing to give more, without targeting the actual abandonment problem.
2. **A two-stage core (~7 questions, ~2-3 minutes, all forced-choice/checkbox) + optional extended tier (~10-11 questions, the qualitative/pricing/prioritization content) is recommended.** The core preserves the campaign's operational mechanism (Q16/17) and its cheapest, highest-value diagnostic signal (Q5, Q7, Q13, Q2/Q3); the extended tier preserves everything else, framed honestly as optional rather than silently dropped.
3. **What's lost if most respondents stop at the core is real and is named above, not minimized** — most importantly, the campaign's own central "why wouldn't you adopt" objective (Q12) and its sharpest forced-prioritization signal (Q15) become optional-tier-only, and Deliverable 4's merit-based pilot selection loses most of its actual evidence base for "Sí" respondents who don't continue. The extended tier's own framing can partially offset this by honestly telling engaged "Sí" respondents that more detail helps their own candidacy — a true, non-manipulative motivational hook, not a way to erase the loss.
4. **Sequence this restructure before, not alongside, §8 point 5's campaign-specific modules** — the core/extended shape should be established on the existing shared instrument now; campaign-specific Section B modules (dependent on Wave 1 video production, not yet feasible per §9) inherit that shape later rather than both being designed simultaneously.
5. **Do not modify the current, live Meta campaign's Google Form.** Apply the restructure to the next recruitment wave, whatever form that takes — this preserves both the current campaign's own data integrity and the precedent both source documents already set for exactly this situation.

**What this section does not do:** it does not authorize `ux-designer`/`brand-guardian` to build the actual core/extended split, does not draft the transition-screen copy between tiers, and does not change `merchant-validation-campaign.md` Deliverable 3 itself. Per the standing Decision Ownership rule, this is a real Business/Product-adjacent call about changing an already-approved, seven-pass-remediated instrument — it's presented here as a clear recommendation with the real tradeoffs named, for the Product Owner to confirm or reject, the same rigor this document's own §1-§10 already apply throughout. If confirmed, the next steps are: `ux-designer` specifies the exact tiered Google Forms structure and the transition-screen copy; `brand-guardian` reviews that copy per its standing consultation trigger for new tonal territory; `reviewer` verifies the restructured instrument still matches every Decision Matrix routing it depends on. **A `knowledge-mentor` consultation on one specific methodological question is recommended but not yet performed, per the standing Consultation Pattern:** whether a two-stage mandatory-core/optional-extended survey structure measurably improves completion rate over a flat-length reduction in established survey-methodology literature, and whether there's a known best practice for how explicitly to frame the "you can stop here" moment without it reading as permission to disengage from the extended tier entirely. Naming the question is this agent's job; routing it is Main's, per the Consultation Pattern's own division of responsibility.

## 12. Architecture Evaluation — Questionnaire Delivery Mechanism (Google Forms vs. Native Alternatives)

**Status: Architecture evaluation only, prepared by `architect` at Product Owner request. Nothing here is authorized to be built. Requires explicit Product Owner sign-off before any implementation, per the standing Approval gate (`company/CLAUDE.md`) — same posture as §11 above.** Scoped strictly to the engineering question — implementation effort, data reliability, analytics possibilities, long-term scalability. The UX/Product-Discovery question (whether the external-Form hop is itself the cause of the 96-views/3-responses gap, as opposed to the questionnaire's length, visibility, or the `demo-mode.md` banner fix already in flight) is `marketing`'s parallel diagnosis (§13, once complete), not duplicated here.

**Hard constraint confirmed directly against the codebase, not assumed:** `product/02c-high-fidelity-prototype/` has no `api/` directory, no `vercel.json`, no server-side code anywhere in the repository. State lives entirely in `localStorage` via `StoreProvider` (`src/domain/store.tsx`), and the only third-party script mounted is `@vercel/analytics` (client-side, `src/main.tsx`) — a static analytics beacon, not a backend. Both `nahui.app` and `demo.nahui.app` deploy as static Vite builds from this one repo. Any option below is evaluated honestly against whether it preserves this, or quietly reintroduces server-side infrastructure by another name.

### 12.1 Option A — Keep Google Forms as-is

**Effort:** zero. **Reliability:** high — Google's own mature, load-tested infrastructure; every submission is durable and visible in the response sheet the moment it lands, with no client-side failure mode to reason about at all. **Analytics:** no source/session attribution (`demo.nahui.app` has no pixel, no backend — already an accepted, named limitation in `company/merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md` §7, worked around today by manual timestamp cross-referencing and, per this document's §8 point 6, a planned self-report screening question); no way to correlate a Form response to a specific prototype session. **Scalability:** fine at this project's own stated confidence-tier discipline (`market-validation.md`'s n=10/20/100/300 bands) — nothing about Google Forms' own capacity is a limiting factor at any volume this validation program is realistically going to reach before a real backend exists.

**What Option A does not solve:** it doesn't change the fact that reaching the questionnaire still requires leaving the app entirely. But that gap is already the subject of an in-flight fix that costs nothing further architecturally — `demo-mode.md`'s 2026-08-18 amendment (persistent Form-reminder banner + welcome-screen expectation-setting copy) targets exactly this, with zero delivery-mechanism change. That fix is UX-side, already reviewed, and does not require touching this section's decision at all.

### 12.2 Option B — Native in-prototype UI, submitting to Google Forms' `formResponse` endpoint (`no-cors`)

**Effort:** low-moderate — a real, working, well-known pattern (hidden fields mapped to `entry.XXXXX` IDs, `fetch(..., {mode: 'no-cors'})`). Genuinely respects the no-backend constraint: 100% client-side, ships as part of the existing static build, no new deploy target.

**Disqualifying reliability problem, not a minor caveat.** `no-cors` means the response is opaque by design — the client can never distinguish success, network failure, or a silently-rejected submission. No error handling is possible in principle, not just in this implementation. This project's every other client-initiated write is designed around `architecture-principles.md` principle 7 (idempotent/keyed retries, so a client can always safely re-attempt and know the result) — this pattern cannot honor that principle at all, because there is no result to read. For a domain write that would be a Blocker on its own; a questionnaire response isn't a domain write, but the same underlying risk — a silent, undetectable loss of exactly the data this whole campaign exists to produce — is worse here in practice, not less severe, given how thin the sample already is (n=2-3 genuine responses to date; every response is individually load-bearing to the confidence-tier discipline `market-validation.md` and `merchant-validation-decision-matrix.md` already hold this project to).

**Compounding, time-specific risk:** the `entry.XXXXX` field IDs are undocumented internal identifiers tied to the Form's exact current structure. This instrument has already been through eight remediation passes (`merchant-validation-campaign.md`), and this very document's own §11 is actively proposing a core/extended restructure of the same Form for the next wave. Hard-coding a submission mapping to a structure that's explicitly under active reconsideration is bad sequencing independent of the general reliability critique — a field reorder, addition, or the §11 tiering split would silently break submission with zero build-time warning, and the failure would look identical to "no one is completing the questionnaire," the same symptom this whole evaluation is responding to.

**Verdict: disqualified.** Not an acceptable trade given this data feeds real product decisions and no error signal exists to either the participant or Nahui when it fails.

### 12.3 Option C — Native in-prototype UI, storing responses elsewhere without a full backend

Three genuinely distinct sub-options, evaluated separately rather than as one bucket:

**C1 — Third-party form-backend service (Formspree-style).** A real HTTP round trip with a real status code — the client can honestly detect success/failure and show a genuine error/retry state, the one thing Option B structurally cannot do; this is the only alternative that can actually honor principle 7's idempotent-retry discipline. Writes nothing to the domain model (same posture `demo-mode.md` already holds for its own device-acknowledgment flag) — no aggregate, no Business/Session/Sale field touched. Real costs, not hidden: a new external vendor/account, which triggers `company/marketing-operating-environment.md`'s standing account-provisioning gate (Product-Owner-level action, not something an agent can autonomously stand up); most free tiers cap monthly submissions (workable at this project's current volume, a real constraint to name if the validation program scales); and it duplicates aggregation/analysis infrastructure Google Forms already gives Nahui for free and that `merchant-validation-decision-matrix.md`'s entire analysis workflow is already built around (a native response sheet, already the shape every Decision Matrix threshold assumes).

**C2 — A Vercel Serverless/Edge Function forwarding to Google Sheets' API (or a datastore).** Named tension, stated plainly rather than papered over: this is architecturally a backend, just a small one, hosted conveniently on the same platform. It requires Nahui to write and maintain real server-side application code, manage a credential (a Sheets API service-account key or OAuth token) as a new secrets-management surface, and design its own error/retry behavior — the exact category of infrastructure "no backend" was meant to keep out of this stage. It could reuse the Google Workspace account Nahui already owns (`marketing-operating-environment.md` #8) rather than requiring a brand-new vendor account, which is a real point in its favor over C1 on the account-provisioning axis specifically — but it does not avoid the deeper point: this is the beginning of `03-build`'s Stage 7 backend-integration work, arriving several stages early, for a marketing-validation instrument.

**C3 — Client-side export/download (no destination, participant-mediated).** Solves reliability differently by shifting the "did it arrive" question onto the participant (a downloaded file she'd have to send, or a pre-filled `mailto:`) rather than solving it. This is strictly dominated by Option A: Option A already satisfies "genuinely zero backend" while also giving Nahui a single, automatically-aggregated response table — C3 adds a manual step for the participant (working against the exact abandonment problem this whole evaluation responds to) and produces scattered, manually-aggregated data instead. Not a real candidate.

**Within Option C, the only sub-option worth taking seriously is C1** — and even C1 is a real answer to a problem the evidence doesn't show exists. Nothing in `demo-mode.md`'s own root-cause analysis, `merchant-validation-campaign-meta-ads.md`, or this document's §0 grounding points at Form-submission failure once a participant reaches and fills out the Form. The diagnosed problem is that the questionnaire isn't being found/tapped — a visibility and hop-friction problem, which `demo-mode.md`'s persistent banner already targets directly at zero further engineering cost. Building a new submission pipeline (C1) or a new backend surface (C2) solves a failure mode that hasn't been observed.

### 12.4 Option D — Recommendation: no delivery-mechanism change

**Architecture-principles.md principle 8 (AI-engineering-lab tiebreaker) — explicitly checked, does not apply.** Step 1 of its gate requires an alternative already justified by a genuine, already-established need; step 2 requires the leading candidates be genuinely comparable in cost/complexity on their own merits before learning value can break a tie. Neither holds here: Option A costs nothing ongoing, while C1/C2 each carry real, non-comparable new cost (a new vendor account and its provisioning gate, or new server-side code and credential management) for a problem this evidence doesn't show exists — the gate fails at step 1 (no genuine need established) and again at step 2 (not cost-comparable to Option A) before learning value could ever be invoked as a tiebreaker. Separately, nothing about a form-relay function touches agentic AI, RAG, orchestration, or any of principle 8's named domains in any real way — it's plumbing, not a candidate for this lens at all. This principle is correctly inapplicable here, not silently skipped.

**Recommendation: keep Google Forms (Option A) for the second campaign. Do not build a native submission pipeline now.** Reasoning, weighed together: Option B is disqualified outright on reliability grounds, made worse by the Form's own active structural evolution (§11). Option C's only serious candidate (C1) is a real, buildable improvement in principle, but it is new engineering effort and a new account-provisioning step spent solving a failure mode nothing in the evidence actually shows — the diagnosed gap (nothing in-app points back to the questionnaire) already has a cheaper, already-reviewed, already-in-flight fix (`demo-mode.md`'s persistent banner) that requires zero change to this section's decision. The right lever for the second campaign is that already-shipped/pending UX-side fix and, separately, §11's own core/extended instrument restructure (a Product Owner decision already pending, unrelated to delivery mechanism) — not a change to how responses are transported.

**Worth revisiting later, specifically if:** a future campaign shows evidence of Form submissions themselves failing or being lost (not merely under-clicked) — the one scenario that would actually justify C1; or the validation program scales past directional/qualitative volumes to a point where session-level attribution becomes decision-blocking rather than a named, accepted limitation, which none of this project's own stated confidence-tier discipline currently requires.

## 13. Is the External Google Forms Transition Itself a Major Abandonment Point? (2026-08-18, Product Owner request)

**Status: diagnosis and recommendation only. Nothing here is authorized to be built. This section evaluates the UX/Product Discovery dimensions of the same question §12 evaluates on architecture grounds — prepared in parallel, reconciled here explicitly against §12's completed verdict rather than left as a separate, unreconciled opinion.**

**Trigger, stated precisely.** ~96 landing-page views on `demo.nahui.app`, 3 questionnaire responses. §11 already evaluated one candidate explanation (the instrument's own length/content) and correctly declined to touch the live Form. This section evaluates a distinct, narrower candidate: whether the *act of leaving the prototype and opening an external, unbranded Google Form* is itself a major, separately-identifiable loss point — independent of what the Form asks once she's in it.

### 13.1 Q1 — Is the app-switch/context-switch itself a meaningful cost?

Plausibly yes, and for a specific, nameable reason rather than a generic "switching is annoying" claim. `demo-mode.md`'s own 2026-08-18 amendment (§3.3's closing sentence, §3.6's banner) was written specifically to establish "esto es una sola experiencia, no dos actividades separadas" — but that framing is delivered entirely in *anticipation*, before she ever taps the link. The moment she actually taps it, three things change simultaneously: (a) she leaves the tab/app context she's been in, (b) she lands on a visually generic, Google-branded interface with none of Nahui's rounded geometry, Coral palette, or Fredoka/Inter typography (`company/brand/brand-guide.md`), and (c) the interaction model itself shifts from "tap through a guided demo" to "fill out a form with sections and a submit button" — a register merchants elsewhere associate with bureaucracy (government forms, bank paperwork), not with a companion product. None of this is hypothetical psychology; it's a direct, observable property of what Google Forms actually looks and feels like relative to the prototype she was just using. Whether it's *large enough* to explain most of the 93 non-responses is a separate, unanswered question (§13.5).

### 13.2 Q2 — Framing problem or structural problem?

Both, but they are not substitutable for each other, and this distinction is the crux of the whole question. **Framing** governs what she expects going in — the welcome-screen sentence and the persistent banner already do real, credible work here, and `demo-mode.md`'s own remediation history (§3.3's "no es un paso aparte," the banner's honest "(8-12 min)" label) shows this project has already taken the framing lever seriously, not left it as an afterthought. **Structure** governs what she actually experiences at the moment of the switch — and copy delivered *before* the switch cannot retroactively soften a visual/interactional discontinuity that happens *at* the switch. Telling her in advance "this is one experience" is a promise; opening a new tab into an unbranded, generic form is the moment that promise is either kept or broken by what she perceives, not by what she was told. **This is a structural problem copy alone cannot fully close** — not because the existing copy work was wrong or wasted (it was the correct, cheap first move, and should stay), but because it operates at a different layer than the one causing the discontinuity. The two are complementary, not competing: even a fully solved structural transition would still benefit from the existing expectation-setting copy — it's not an either/or.

### 13.3 Q3 — Would a native, in-prototype questionnaire plausibly reduce abandonment?

Plausibly, directionally — consistent with the Aesthetic-Usability Effect and Peak-end Rule already surfaced in §4's `knowledge-mentor` consultation (a coherent, branded, single-tab experience should read as less effortful and produce a more favorable retrospective read than a broken one) — **but this is a hypothesis, not a fact this document can assert as established.** No evidence currently isolates "she abandoned because of the transition" from "she abandoned because of the questionnaire's content" (§13.5 states exactly why). It would also be a new, untested intervention in its own right: a native questionnaire is not risk-free by construction — a poorly executed one (visually inconsistent, technically broken, or simply relocating the same 17 questions into a new frame without addressing perceived length) could fail to move completion at all, or move it for reasons unrelated to the transition question this section is actually about. **The honest position:** removing the transition is a defensible, theory-backed bet worth taking if it can be done reliably — but §12 has now closed off the specific mechanism (Option B) that would have made this cheap, on reliability grounds independent of anything in this section's own reasoning. That reframes this from "should we build this" to "not now, not this way" — see §13.6.

### 13.4 Q4 — Does Google Forms' own UI inflate perceived effort independent of actual length?

Plausible, and distinguishable in principle from Q1/Q2's transition-cost question even though it's easy to conflate the two. Google's default Forms rendering carries no built-in progress indicator, no time estimate (this project already had to add one manually to the banner precisely because "the ask might read as smaller/more skippable than it is" — the inverse problem, but evidence the team already suspects Forms under-communicates on its own), and a visual register (gray/white, generic sans-serif, numbered sections) that reads as institutional rather than warm. Two respondents' free-text answers (§0) — "no puedo meter tanta ropa porque es demasiada" and the puzzle vendor's stall — are both about *content* effort inside the prototype, not about the Form's own presentation, so existing evidence doesn't yet speak to this specific question either way. This stays a named, plausible, unconfirmed contributor, not a demonstrated one.

### 13.5 Q5 — The honest attribution question: what can and can't be concluded from 96 views / 3 responses

**Nothing in the current data distinguishes the transition-cost hypothesis from the content-length hypothesis, and it's important to say precisely why, not just assert that it's "hard to tell."** The 96→3 funnel collapses at least four distinct, unobserved steps into one aggregate number:

1. Of the 96 landing-page views, how many ever reached a screen where the banner/CTA was visible at all (i.e., completed enough of Onboarding to reach `pass-through`)? Unknown — no event exists for this.
2. Of those, how many tapped the banner/link at all (i.e., attempted to open the Form)? Unknown — no click event exists on the banner itself.
3. Of those who opened the Form, how many started answering versus closed the tab immediately on arrival? Unknown — Google Forms' free tier reports completed responses only; it does not expose partial/abandoned response data.
4. Of those who started, how many abandoned partway due to length/content versus completed? Unknown for the same reason as (3).

Every one of these four steps is a distinct place volume could be lost, and each maps to a different explanation this section and §11 have separately proposed. **The existing evidence at n=2 genuine respondents actually demonstrates that at least two of these loss points are real and distinct, which is itself the strongest thing this section can honestly say:** the puzzle vendor (§0) stalled *inside the prototype*, before ever reaching a point where the Form question is even relevant — a loss at step 1, unrelated to Forms entirely. The 30-product vendor reached and completed the Form. Two data points cannot establish *proportions*, but they do establish that the funnel has more than one real bottleneck already, which means any single explanation ("it's the transition" or "it's the questionnaire") offered without qualification is almost certainly incomplete on its own, whichever one turns out to dominate.

**What would actually distinguish these explanations, if it became available:**
- **A click/tap event on the banner's questionnaire row**, logged client-side — this is the one piece of instrumentation compatible with §12's own hard no-backend constraint (a call to `@vercel/analytics`, already mounted in `src/main.tsx`, or an equally lightweight client-side counter) — it would split step 1 from step 2, telling us whether people are reaching the CTA and declining to tap it (a framing/motivation problem) versus never reaching it at all (a prototype-completion problem, unrelated to Forms). This does not require any of Options B/C/D from §12 — it is compatible with keeping Option A exactly as `architect` recommended.
- **An "opened the Form" versus "submitted the Form" distinction** — this is precisely the capability §12 found unavailable without a real backend (Option C1 at minimum, itself judged a solution to an unobserved failure mode). Given §12's verdict, this specific piece of evidence is not available under the currently-recommended architecture and would only become available if a future round's evidence justified revisiting that call.
- **A short self-report question inside the questionnaire itself** ("¿qué tanto te costó pasar del prototipo al cuestionario?") could add color for the respondents who *do* complete it, but this cannot diagnose the 93 who didn't — it only ever samples people who already got past the exact obstacle in question, the same survivorship-bias caveat this document already names elsewhere (§4).

**Conclusion, stated plainly:** the transition-cost hypothesis and the content-length hypothesis are both live, both plausible, and currently indistinguishable with the data on hand. Treating either one as confirmed would overstate what 96/3 can support. The single highest-value next step, compatible with §12's own architecture verdict and requiring no change to the Form or the delivery mechanism, is adding a cheap, count-only click event on the existing banner's questionnaire row.

### 13.6 UX / Product Discovery read on Options A-D, reconciled against §12's completed verdict

Judged on UX continuity and Product Discovery value, now read against §12's own completed technical findings rather than as an independent, unreconciled preference:

- **Option A (keep Google Forms as-is):** does not address the structural discontinuity named in §13.2. On its own, from a pure UX-continuity standpoint, this is the weakest option of the four — but §12 confirms it is the only one with no disqualifying technical cost, and the diagnosed gap it leaves open (nothing in-app pointing back to the questionnaire) is already the subject of the already-shipped/pending banner fix, which addresses the *framing* half of §13.2's distinction at zero further cost. **Recommended for the next campaign, on this section's own UX grounds too** — not because the structural gap isn't real, but because the one option that would have closed it cleanly (Option B) is independently disqualified on reliability grounds this section has no standing to override, and the remaining candidate that could close it (C1) is solving a failure mode §12 found no evidence for.
- **Option B (client-side POST to Forms' public endpoint):** would have been the strongest candidate on UX/Discovery grounds specifically — no tab switch, no visual-language break, continuous with the branded experience the copy already promises. **This section's earlier-drafted preference for this option is superseded by §12's disqualification on reliability grounds** (opaque `no-cors` submission, no error detection, undocumented field IDs tied to a Form actively being restructured per §11) — a technical risk this section is not positioned to weigh against and does not dispute. Noted for the record as the option that would have most directly closed the structural gap, had it been technically sound.
- **Option C (native UI, alternate storage):** only C1 (a real backend-equivalent form service) offers genuine in-app continuity; §12 judges it a real but currently unjustified cost, since no evidence shows Form-submission failure as the actual problem. From a pure UX-continuity standpoint C1 would help — but building it now would be solving for a hypothesis (§13.3) this document has explicitly not confirmed, at a real cost §12 has already named. Not recommended for the next campaign, consistent with §12.
- **Option D (architect's actual recommendation — no delivery-mechanism change):** on UX/Discovery grounds, this converges with §12's own conclusion once reconciled: the structural gap named in §13.2 stays open for now, not because it isn't real, but because the one cheap, reliable way to close it doesn't currently exist, and the honest next step is measuring the gap (§13.5) before spending real engineering effort assuming its size.

**Recommendation, reconciled:** keep Google Forms (Option A) for the next campaign, matching `architect`'s §12 verdict — this section's own UX analysis does not override that on technical grounds it has no standing to weigh. What this section adds, not already present in §12: (1) the structural discontinuity named in §13.2 is real and worth revisiting once a technically sound way to close it exists (a future backend stage, or a Formspree-style service if evidence later justifies its cost); (2) the single most valuable action available right now, compatible with §12's constraint, is the lightweight click-event instrumentation named in §13.5 — it costs nothing, requires no delivery-mechanism change, and is the only concrete step that would let this section's own open question (transition vs. content) actually get answered with the next wave's data instead of re-argued from the same thin evidence.

### 13.7 Summary

1. **Both the transition-cost and content-length explanations are plausible; neither is confirmed by current evidence.** §11 already declined to treat length as settled; this section applies the identical discipline to the transition hypothesis.
2. **The transition is a real structural discontinuity, not only a framing gap** — the existing welcome-screen/banner copy work was the correct first move and should stay, but it operates at a different layer (expectation) than the one causing the discontinuity (actual visual/interactional break at the moment of the switch), and cannot fully substitute for closing that gap structurally.
3. **A native, in-prototype questionnaire is a defensible, theory-backed bet, not a proven fix — and §12 has independently disqualified the one cheap way to build it (Option B) on reliability grounds.** The remaining way to actually close the structural gap (C1) is a real cost `architect` judges unjustified by current evidence. This section does not dispute either finding.
4. **The single highest-value next step is cheap, delivery-mechanism-agnostic instrumentation, not a redesign decision** — a click event on the existing banner's questionnaire row, compatible with keeping Option A exactly as §12 recommends, is what would actually let this attribution question be answered going forward instead of re-argued from the same thin evidence.
5. **Recommendation, reconciled with §12: keep Google Forms (Option A) for the next campaign.** This section's UX preference for a native, in-app alternative (Option B) is superseded by §12's technical disqualification; the structural gap it would have closed stays a named, open, worth-revisiting limitation — not solved now, not ignored either.
